From the driver's seat, a car is basically free. The gas tank is mysteriously full. Insurance is something parents complain about. Oil changes happen to other people.

Then your teenager gets a license, and the costs suddenly have a new driver attached to them. This is a good moment for a conversation about money, and it's one that can make them a more careful driver, too.

## The real numbers

Start by showing them the bill. Actual numbers, from your own household if you're comfortable sharing them, or national figures if you're not.

- **Owning a car.** AAA estimates the total cost of owning and operating a new vehicle at [about $11,577 a year](https://newsroom.aaa.com/2025/09/aaa-new-vehicle-costs-drop-to-11577/), counting fuel, maintenance, insurance, depreciation and fees. An older used car costs less, but not nothing.
- **Adding a teen to insurance.** For a 16-year-old, adding them to a parent's policy raises it by [about $3,900 to $4,500 a year on average](https://www.carinsurance.com/Articles/car-insurance-16-year-old.aspx).
- **A ticket.** A speeding ticket raises rates by [an average of 38% at renewal](https://www.carinsurance.com/how-much-insurance-goes-up-for-speeding-ticket.aspx), for years.

Teenagers are often genuinely surprised. That surprise is the point.

## Should they pay part of it?

That's your family's call, and there's no right answer. Some families cover everything so a teen can focus on school. Others expect the teen to cover gas, or a share of insurance, or the deductible if something happens.

A few approaches that tend to work:

- **Gas is theirs.** Simple, visible and directly tied to how much they drive.
- **A fixed monthly share of insurance.** Even a small amount makes the cost real.
- **The increase is theirs.** If a ticket or an at-fault crash raises the premium, they cover some or all of the difference. It connects the consequence to the cause.
- **The deductible is theirs.** If they cause a crash, they owe the deductible, paid over time if needed.

Whatever you choose, write it into your [driving agreement](/parent-teen-driving-agreement/) so it's settled before the first bill, not argued over after.

> A teenager who pays for the gas notices how much driving they do. That's not a bad thing to notice.

## Why it matters beyond money

There's a safety angle here. A teen who knows that a ticket costs their family hundreds or thousands of dollars, and who will feel some of that, has one more reason to drive the speed limit. A teen who pays for gas thinks twice about the pointless cruise around town at 11 p.m. with friends.

That's not a substitute for practice and good rules. It's just another way of making the stakes real without anyone getting hurt.

## Keep it fair

If they contribute, make sure the car and the rules match. A teen paying for part of the insurance on a car will reasonably feel they have some say in when they use it. That's fine, inside the agreement you've made.

And make sure the money doesn't get in the way of safety. A teen should never avoid calling you after a crash because they're worried about the deductible, or skip a needed repair because it's their turn to pay. Say that out loud.

## This week

Sit down with your teen and the actual insurance bill. Show them what it was before they were added and what it is now. Then ask what share they think is fair. Their answer may surprise you.
