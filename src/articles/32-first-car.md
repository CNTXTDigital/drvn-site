At some point the conversation turns from "can I drive the car?" to "can I have a car?" And for most families, the answer comes down to what's affordable, what's available, and what the kid thinks looks good.

I've told the story of [my oldest son's first car](/most-dangerous-thing-teen-drivers-license/) elsewhere. It is not a model to follow.

Here's what I'd want to know if I were doing it again.

## The pattern to avoid

The riskiest drivers on the road tend to end up in the least protective cars. IIHS looked at teen drivers killed in crashes and found [28% were in a micro, mini or small car, compared with 19% of adult drivers, and their vehicles were about 250 pounds lighter on average](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/teens-are-the-riskiest-drivers-but-they-drive-the-least-safe-vehicles). Teens were also more likely to be driving cars 11 to 15 years old.

That makes complete sense economically. The cheapest car is usually small and old, and a first car is usually the cheapest car. It's just the wrong place to save money.

## IIHS's four rules

The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety keeps its advice simple. [Its guidance for a teen's vehicle](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/teenagers):

1. **Stay away from high horsepower.** Power encourages speed, and a new driver doesn't need the temptation. If a model comes with a bigger engine option, stick to the base engine.
2. **Not too small, not too big.** Small cars protect worse in a crash. Large SUVs and pickups are harder to handle and take longer to stop.
3. **Electronic stability control is a must.** It helps keep the car from sliding out on a curve or in an emergency swerve. It's been standard on new cars for years; on an older used car, check.
4. **The best crash test ratings you can afford.**

## Finding one you can afford

IIHS publishes a list of [safe vehicles for teens](https://www.iihs.org/ratings/safe-vehicles-for-teens), updated each year, organized by price. The 2026 list includes dozens of used vehicles under $10,000 and more under $20,000. Every car on it weighs more than 2,750 pounds, has stability control, and earned a good rating in a demanding front crash test.

Start there. Then narrow it down by what's for sale near you.

## What else to check

- **Tires.** A safe car on bald tires isn't a safe car. Budget for new ones if they're worn.
- **Brakes and maintenance records.** Have a mechanic look at any used car before you buy.
- **Recalls.** Enter the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) before you buy, and fix anything open.
- **Insurance.** Get a quote before you buy. The same teen can cost very different amounts to insure in different cars.
- **Their stuff, not just the car.** A phone mount that keeps navigation out of their hands, and driving focus set up on the phone from day one.

## Whose car is it?

There's a quieter question too. A car that's "theirs" tends to go more places, with more people, at more hours, than a car they borrow from you. Some families find it easier to keep the early months of the handoff on track when the teen drives a family car and asks to use it.

That's not a rule. But if you're on the fence about buying, it's a reason to wait a few months.

## This week

Look up your teen's current car, or the one you're considering, on the IIHS list. If it's not there, find out why. Then decide whether that's a reason you can live with.
