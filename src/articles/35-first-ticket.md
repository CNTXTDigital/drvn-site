At some point, your teenager will come home quieter than usual, holding a piece of paper they'd very much like you not to see.

A first ticket is common, and it isn't the end of the world. It's also not nothing. For a new driver, a ticket can cost more and change more than it would for you, and it's worth understanding both before you decide how to handle it.

## What it can cost

The ticket itself is usually the smallest part. The bigger cost shows up at insurance renewal.

Across all drivers, a speeding ticket raises insurance rates by [an average of 38% at renewal](https://www.carinsurance.com/how-much-insurance-goes-up-for-speeding-ticket.aspx), according to CarInsurance.com's 2026 analysis. Teens often see larger increases, and the range between insurers is enormous. On a policy that already went up a lot when you added a teenager, a percentage increase on top of that can add up quickly. More on the baseline in [What a Teen Driver Actually Costs](/what-a-teen-driver-costs/).

## What it can change

This is the part many families don't expect. During the provisional or intermediate license, a violation can affect the license itself.

- **Iowa** [delays the move to the next license stage by 12 months for each conviction, crash or restriction violation](https://iowadot.gov/drivers-licenses-ids/get-or-renew-drivers-licenses-ids-permits/under-18-permit-license-or-id/intermediate-license).
- **California** can [restrict or suspend a provisional license after collisions or violations in the first year](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/laws-and-rules-of-the-road-cont2/). Two at-fault crashes or convictions means 30 days of driving only with a licensed adult 25 or older.
- **Ohio** says a conviction in the first six months [may require a parent or guardian to ride along for six months or until the driver turns 17](https://www.bmv.ohio.gov/dl-gdl.aspx).

Your state's rules will be different. Look them up before deciding what to do, because they change the math.

> For a new driver, the ticket is the cheap part.

## Fight it, pay it, or traffic school?

That depends on the ticket and your state, and it's your family's call. A few things worth knowing:

- **Paying the ticket is usually pleading guilty.** It goes on the record, with whatever that means for points, insurance and license restrictions.
- **Some states and courts allow traffic school or a defensive driving course** to dismiss the ticket or keep points off the record. Eligibility varies, and some states don't allow it for provisional drivers. Ask the court before paying.
- **If you think the ticket was wrong,** contesting it is a legitimate option. If you think it was right, it's worth letting your teen hear you say so.

I'm not a lawyer, and the rules vary a lot by state and by court. If the stakes are high, a local traffic attorney can tell you what's realistic.

## The conversation

A ticket is a much better teacher than a near-miss, because nobody got hurt and the consequence is real.

So skip the lecture. Ask them to walk you through what happened. Ask what they'd do differently. Then agree on what happens now, and have your teen do the work: read the ticket, call the court, find out the options, sign up for the course if there is one. If the insurance goes up, decide together how much of that is theirs to pay.

If your family has a [driving agreement](/parent-teen-driving-agreement/), this is what the "if a rule is broken" section is for. Use what you agreed to, calmly, and then move on.

## The bigger point

Most first tickets are for speed. If that's what happened, it's worth a few drives together to work on choosing speed for conditions, not just for the sign. There's more on that in [Speed: Teaching the Number That Isn't on the Sign](/teen-drivers-speeding/).

A first ticket handled well is one of the most useful things that can happen to a new driver. It's real enough to matter and small enough to recover from.

## This week

If your teen has a provisional license, look up what a violation would mean in your state, and tell them. It's much better for them to learn the rule from you than from a police officer.
