When a family signs up for driver's ed, there's a quiet sense of relief. Somebody who does this for a living is going to teach my kid to drive. Box checked.

I understand the feeling. It's also where a lot of families relax too early.

Driver's ed is genuinely useful. It just can't do the whole job, and it was never designed to.

## What driver's ed does well

A good course teaches the rules of the road, the signs, the right-of-way questions nobody's parents can answer correctly, and the basics of handling a car. A good instructor catches habits a parent misses, and can often get through to a teenager in a way a parent can't. Anyone who has tried to explain a four-way stop to their own kid knows why that matters.

And it seems to help. A University of Nebraska study followed about 150,000 teen drivers. Compared with those who took driver's ed, [teens who skipped it were 75% more likely to get a ticket, 24% more likely to be in a crash with injuries or deaths, and 16% more likely to crash at all](https://news.unl.edu/newsrooms/today/article/study-driver-s-ed-significantly-reduces-teen-crashes-tickets) in their first two years of driving.

That's a real effect. It's worth being honest about the limits too: families who choose driver's ed may differ from families who don't, and [NHTSA still lists pre-licensure driver ed among approaches that need more evaluation](https://www.nhtsa.gov/book/countermeasures-that-work/young-drivers/countermeasures/unproven-further-evaluation/pre-licensure). So, useful, not magic.

## What it can't do

Look at the size of it. A typical course is a few dozen hours of classroom or online instruction, plus a handful of hours behind the wheel with an instructor. Iowa, where I live now, requires [30 hours of classroom and 6 in the car](/guide/iowa/). Ohio requires [24 classroom hours and 8 behind the wheel](/guide/ohio/).

Six or eight hours with an instructor is a great start. It is not the experience it takes to become a capable driver. That comes from hundreds of ordinary decisions on real roads: a left turn across traffic, a merge in the rain, a dark two-lane road with a deer somewhere in it. No course has the time to give that to every student.

Which is why every state also asks for supervised practice with a parent. The system assumes both halves happen.

> Driver's ed teaches. Practice with you turns it into experience.

## How to make it count

**Time it so the lessons line up with your practice.** If the course covers highway driving in week three, drive on a highway that weekend. Practice right after instruction makes both stick.

**Ask the instructor what they saw.** After behind-the-wheel sessions, ask one question: "What should we work on?" Instructors notice things parents don't, and most are glad to tell you.

**Do the homework with them, at least once.** Sit in on part of the online course or flip through the materials. You'll probably find a rule or two you've been breaking for twenty years. It also means you're teaching the same thing in the car.

**Don't let the certificate end the practice.** Completing the course doesn't mean the practice requirement is met, and it definitely doesn't mean your teen is ready. The certificate is one item on the list.

## If your state doesn't require it

Some states make driver's ed optional or only require it for younger teens. You can still do it. Given the Nebraska numbers and what it costs compared to a single fender bender, it's one of the cheaper safety decisions you'll make. Your [state guide](/guide/) shows what's required where you live.

If cost or scheduling is the obstacle, an online course can handle the classroom part on your teen's schedule. ## The part that's still yours

Driver's ed gives your teen a foundation and a professional's eye. You give them the volume, the variety and the repetition: the night drives, the rain, the unfamiliar highway, the hundred left turns.

Neither one is enough alone. Together, they're the best version of this process anyone has come up with so far.
