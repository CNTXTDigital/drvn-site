Choosing a driver's ed course usually goes like this. Somebody's friend used a place. It was fine. You sign up.

That's not a terrible method. But a few minutes of checking up front can save you a course your state won't accept, a behind-the-wheel waitlist that pushes the road test back two months, or a format that your particular teenager will never actually finish.

## First, find out what your state requires

This is the step that matters most, because the rules vary a lot.

Some states require driver's ed before the permit. Some require it before the license. Some only require it for younger teens, and some don't require it at all. Many split it into a classroom part and a behind-the-wheel part with a licensed instructor. A few have very specific rules: Texas, for example, allows [parent-taught driver education](/guide/texas/), and Arizona lets an approved course [waive the road test](/teen-road-test-day/).

Your [state guide](/guide/) lists what's required where you live, with a link to the official source. Read it before you shop. Whatever you choose has to be approved by your state for teen licensing, and the certificate has to be the kind your licensing office accepts.

## Online or in person?

For the classroom part, most states now accept an approved online course. A few don't, so check first. The behind-the-wheel part, where a state requires it, happens in a car with an instructor either way.

**Online works well when:**
- Your teen is self-directed enough to finish something without a teacher waiting on them.
- Schedules are packed and a fixed class time won't work.
- There's no good driving school nearby, or the local ones have long waits.
- You'd like to see the material yourself and talk it through at home.

**In person works well when:**
- Your teen does better with a set time, a room and a teacher.
- They'd benefit from hearing other students' questions.
- The school bundles classroom and behind-the-wheel time, which can make scheduling simpler.

Plenty of families combine them: online classroom, then behind-the-wheel lessons with a local school.

## What to check before you pay

**State approval.** Is the course approved for teen licensing in your state? It should say so clearly. If it doesn't, ask.

**What the certificate covers.** Classroom only, or classroom and behind-the-wheel? Is there a separate fee for the certificate or for sending it to the state?

**Behind-the-wheel availability.** If your state requires instructor time, how soon can your teen actually get it? In some areas, the wait is the bottleneck.

**Total cost.** Course fee, behind-the-wheel hours, certificate, any testing fees. Compare the full number, not the advertised one.

**Timing.** Can your teen finish before the date that matters, whether that's the permit test or the road test?

**For a driving school, a few questions worth asking:**
- How many hours will my teen actually spend driving, not observing?
- Will the instructor tell me what to work on afterward?
- What roads do lessons cover? Highway? Night?
- What happens if a lesson gets canceled?

## Whatever you choose

The course is the start of the process, not the bulk of it. Even a great course adds up to a few dozen hours. The practice you do together afterward is where the experience comes from. More on that in [Is Driver's Ed Enough?](/is-drivers-ed-enough/)

So pick the course that your teen will actually finish, on time, with a certificate your state accepts. Then get in the car.
