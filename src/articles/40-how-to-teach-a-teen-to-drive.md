<div class="short-answer"><span class="sa-k">The short answer</span><p>Teach your teen to drive in stages. Start in an empty parking lot, move to quiet streets until the controls are automatic, then add busy roads, highways, night, rural roads and bad weather. Aim for about 70 hours of supervised practice, and spend the last few months handing the decisions over to them.</p></div>

When my oldest son got his permit, I got in the car with no plan. I knew the state wanted fifty hours. I figured fifty hours would take care of itself.

It did, technically. Most of those hours were on the same eight miles of road between our house and everywhere we already needed to go.

Here's the plan I wish I'd had. It's six steps, in order. None of them are complicated. The hard part is doing them in sequence instead of all at once in the month before the road test.

## Before the first drive: three things to settle

**Know your state's rules.** Permit age, required hours, night hours and passenger limits vary a lot. Ohio asked my family for 50 hours, 10 of them at night. Iowa, where I live now, asks for 20, with 2 at night. Your [state guide](/guide/) has the details.

**Make a list of places to drive.** Six headings: residential streets, busy commercial roads, highway, rural roads, night and bad weather. Under each, write two or three real places near you. The reason is in [The Six Environments](/six-driving-environments-teen-practice/).

**Agree on how you'll talk in the car.** One instruction at a time, the same words every time, and the post-mortem happens in the driveway, not at the stop sign. [Feedback Without a Fight](/driving-feedback-without-a-fight/) covers the rest.

## Step 1: Learn the car in an empty parking lot

**The first drive should be in a big, empty lot, and it should teach the car, not driving.**

Seat, mirrors, pedals, lights, wipers. Then creeping forward with no gas, stopping smoothly, slow loops, gentle turns and backing up in a straight line. Twenty or thirty minutes is plenty. Stop on something that went well.

**Move on when:** stopping and steering are smooth, and neither of you is gripping anything. Usually two or three sessions. [The First Drive](/teen-first-drive/) walks through it.

## Step 2: Quiet residential streets until the controls are automatic

**Stay on low-traffic streets until operating the car stops taking all their attention.**

Lane position, smooth braking, stop signs, turning at intersections, and looking farther ahead than the hood. Twenty minutes three times a week beats two hours on a Sunday.

This step feels slow. It's the foundation for everything after it, because a teen still thinking about the pedals has nothing left over for traffic.

**Move on when:** they can drive a familiar three-mile route while you say nothing at all, and that isn't stressful for either of you.

## Step 3: Busy roads, where judgment starts

**Once control is automatic, move to multi-lane commercial roads, where the real skill is reading other drivers.**

This is where a large share of new-driver crashes happen. Practice choosing gaps, especially [left turns across traffic](/teaching-left-turns/), keeping a safe [following distance](/following-distance-teen-drivers/), lane changes, and cars pulling out of parking lots without warning.

Start asking "what's the risk here?" at random moments, and make them answer. Not a quiz. A habit.

**Move on when:** they pick safe gaps without being told, and they start pointing out hazards before you do.

## Step 4: Highways, in order

**Teach highway driving in steps, starting with a quiet interchange and building to rush hour.**

A quiet rural on-ramp first. Then a suburban one. Then one with trucks. Then real traffic. That's at least four sessions, spread over a few weeks. The on-ramp is for [matching speed](/teaching-highway-merging/), not for stopping and waiting.

This is the step parents postpone the longest. It's also why so many teenagers make their first real highway drive alone.

**Move on when:** merging, changing lanes and exiting feel routine, at highway speed, with traffic around them.

## Step 5: Night, rural roads and bad weather

**These are the conditions most families skip, and they're where inexperience is most dangerous.**

**Night.** Start at dusk on familiar roads, then unfamiliar ones, then rain after dark. More than a third of teen driving deaths happen between 9 p.m. and 3 a.m., according to [IIHS](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/teenagers). Whatever your state requires at night, do much more. [Night Driving](/teen-night-driving/) has a plan.
<p class="src">Source: <a href="https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/teenagers">IIHS, Teenagers</a></p>

**Rural roads.** No shoulder, changing surfaces and hills that hide what's coming. Nothing you need is out there, so it only happens if you plan a loop on purpose.

**Bad weather.** You can't schedule it, so take it when it comes. The first hard rain of the fall is a lesson, not a reason to cancel. [Rain, Snow and Ice on Purpose](/weather-driving-practice/) explains how to start.

**Move on when:** they've driven in each condition more than once, not just survived it once.

## Step 6: Hand over the decisions

**In the last few months, the goal is to take yourself out of the driving while you're still in the car.**

Let them navigate somewhere new with no directions from you. Do whole drives where you say nothing unless it's a safety issue, and talk about it afterward. Let them own the trip: the route, when to leave, where to park.

This is the hardest step for parents, which is mostly the point.

**You're close when:** they drive forty-five minutes somewhere new, in traffic, and you didn't speak and didn't need to. [Are You Still Driving From the Passenger Seat?](/stop-coaching-teen-driver/) is the honest check.

## How many hours of practice does a teen need?

**Use 70 hours as a target. That's the [IIHS recommendation](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/teenagers), and it's more than most states require.**

But the number matters less than what's in it. In a [randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24957844/), families given a plan that spread their teen's practice across more kinds of driving saw failure on an on-road safety test drop from 15% to 6%. They didn't drive more. They drove in more places.
<p class="src">Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24957844/">CHOP TeenDrivingPlan randomized trial, JAMA Pediatrics, 2014</a> · <a href="/evidence/#teen-driving-plan">Evidence Library</a></p>

And count honestly. Years after my oldest, our log showed my younger son had 15 hours. My wife was sure it was more. "It feels like fifty," she said. It does feel like fifty. It doesn't perform like fifty. [The 50-Hour Myth](/50-hour-myth-driving-practice/) is the full story.

## How long does it take to teach a teen to drive?

**Plan on most of a year.** The steps aren't a weekend each. Control usually takes the first two or three months, judgment the next few, and the hard environments need the most calendar time because night and weather don't arrive on schedule. [The Permit Year, Month by Month](/permit-year-practice-plan/) lays it out.

If you're starting late, cut repetition, not environments. Fewer sessions per step is fine. Skipping night, weather, highway or rural roads isn't.

## How do you know they're ready to drive alone?

**Passing the road test means they're eligible. It doesn't mean they're ready.** The state sets eligibility. You set readiness.

Look for five things: smooth control, hazard awareness without prompting, good judgment on speed and gaps, real experience across conditions, and drives where you aren't making the decisions anymore.
