Here is something nobody says out loud: by the time your kid is old enough to drive, you are desperate for them to drive.

You have been the family transportation department for fifteen or sixteen years. School, practice, the friend’s house, the other friend’s house, the forgotten cleats, the 9:40 pickup from a thing that was supposed to end at 9. Then one day the state says this kid is eligible to drive themselves, and every tired cell in your body hears “ready.”

Those are not the same word. And you are the worst-positioned person in the world to tell them apart, because you badly want them to be the same.

> The state sets eligibility. You set readiness.

The state’s job is a floor: an age, a number of logged hours, a written test, a short drive with an examiner. Once your teen clears it, the state is done. Whether this particular kid can handle a dark two-lane road with a friend in the passenger seat is a question it never asks. It can’t. That one is yours.

## Why this is the decision that matters most

You’d think the risky part of all this is the permit year, when a fifteen-year-old is learning which pedal is which. It isn’t. You’re sitting right there.

The risky part starts the day you’re not. [A study led by the National Institutes of Health](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/news/071018-teen-driving) put recording equipment in the cars of teenagers through their permit year and into their first year of driving alone. In the first three months after the license, their rate of crashes and near-crashes was about eight times what it had been in the last three months of supervised driving. And it [didn’t come down over the rest of that first year](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2018.04.012).

Read that again with your own driveway in mind. Same kid, same car, same roads. The only thing that changed was that you got out.

So when you decide your teenager is ready to drive alone, you are making the single biggest safety decision of this entire process. Most of us make it by default.

## I let the test decide

With my oldest, “is he ready?” never came up as its own question. He passed the road test. That was the answer. I didn’t think about it beyond that.

I don’t say that because something terrible happened. I say it because it’s how almost everybody does it, and because it means a fifteen-minute drive around the licensing office made a decision that I should have made over months.

## What ready actually looks like

Readiness isn’t a feeling. You will never feel completely fine watching them back out alone the first time, and if you wait for that feeling you’ll be driving them to college. It’s something you can watch for. Here’s how I’d look.

Count what you say. Take one ordinary drive and keep a quiet tally of every time you speak up to help. “Watch that car.” “Slow down.” “You’ve got a stop sign.” Early on that number is huge, and it should be, because every one of those is you doing part of the driving. Readiness is when that number gets close to zero on a normal drive, and stays there. Not because you bit your tongue. Because there was nothing to say.

Ask them what they see. Every so often, ask, “What are you watching right now?” A ready driver answers fast and specifically: the kid on the bike, the brake lights two cars up, the truck that’s been drifting toward the line. A driver who isn’t ready says “the road,” which is technically correct and tells you everything.

Let them navigate. Pick somewhere they’ve never driven to and stay quiet. A lot of teens who are perfectly smooth on their usual eight miles fall apart when they also have to figure out where they’re going. Alone, they will always have to figure out where they’re going.

Watch the second mistake. Everybody makes the first one. Readiness is what happens next. A ready driver misses a turn and takes the next one. A driver who isn’t ready misses a turn and makes a sudden lane change to save it.

Ask whether they’d say no. Heavy rain, a friend who wants to drive somewhere at midnight, a night when they’re exhausted. Can they tell you, without being prompted, when a drive is beyond them? Judgment about when not to drive is part of driving. It just doesn’t show up on any test.

> The best sign they’re ready isn’t how well they drive while you’re coaching. It’s how little coaching they need.

## Ready for what?

This is the part I wish I’d understood the first time: ready isn’t a yes-or-no question. It’s ready for what.

A teen can be completely ready to drive themselves to school at 7:30 in the morning and nowhere near ready to drive home from a party at 11 with three friends in the car. Those are different drives. Treat them that way.

So don’t hand over the keys all at once. Hand them over in pieces. Start with the familiar routes in daylight. Then other daylight trips. Then a highway on their own. Then dark. Then a friend in the car. Keep doing the hard stuff, like night, rain or unfamiliar highways, together for a while after the license, even though the state says you don’t have to.

Your state probably does a version of this already with passenger limits and a curfew. Those are the legal minimum. Nothing stops you from running a stricter version of your own, and your teenager will survive it. They will also complain, which is how you know it’s working.

## If they’re not ready yet

Then they’re not ready yet. The age on the license is the earliest your teen can go, not a deadline.

This is a hard conversation, especially if their friends are already driving. Make it about specific things, not about them. “You’re not ready” lands as a verdict. “I want to see three more night drives and one trip on the interstate where I don’t say anything” is a plan with an end date. Teenagers can work with a plan. Honestly, so can parents.

And if you realize the gap is because the practice never covered the hard stuff, that isn’t a failure. It’s the most common thing in the world. The fix is the same as it always was, and it’s laid out in [The 50-Hour Myth](/50-hour-myth-driving-practice/): more drives, in more places, with one thing to work on each time.

## This week

If your teen is within a few months of the license, do one thing: take a normal drive together and count. Every prompt, every “careful,” every hand that drifts toward the dashboard. Don’t tell them you’re doing it.

That number is the most honest readiness test you have. Nobody from the state is ever going to ask you for it. That’s exactly why it’s worth knowing.
